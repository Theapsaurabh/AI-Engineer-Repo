## 📖 Overview of Statistics

Statistics is the science of **collecting, analysing, interpreting, and presenting data**. In AI/ML, it is the backbone for:

- **Understanding data** before modeling,
- **Feature engineering**,
- **Model evaluation**,
- **Validating assumptions**,
- **Making data‑driven decisions**.

It is broadly divided into **Descriptive Statistics** (summarising data) and **Inferential Statistics** (drawing conclusions from data).

---

## 📊 Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics summarise and organise data so that it becomes understandable. They describe the **main features** of a dataset.

### 📈 Measures of Central Tendency

These measures locate the “centre” of the data.

| Measure | Definition | Formula | When to Use |
|---------|------------|---------|-------------|
| **Mean** | Arithmetic average | $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i}{n}$$ | Symmetric distribution, no outliers |
| **Median** | Middle value when sorted | – sort and pick middle | Skewed data, presence of outliers |
| **Mode** | Most frequent value | – frequency count | Categorical data, multimodal distributions |

#### 📝 Worked Example – Student Heights

> Data: **155, 160, 165, 170, 175, 180** cm

- **Mean** = (155+160+165+170+175+180) / 6 = **167.5** cm  
- **Median** = (165+170)/2 = **167.5** cm (since even n)  
- **Mode** = none – all values appear once.

**Interpretation**: The average height is about 167.5 cm, and the middle value is also 167.5 cm, indicating a symmetric distribution.

---

### 📉 Measures of Dispersion

These describe the **spread** or **variability** of the data.

| Measure | Definition | Formula | Interpretation |
|---------|------------|---------|----------------|
| **Range** | Max – Min | – | Sensitive to outliers |
| **Variance** | Average squared deviation from mean | $$\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{N}$$ (population) | Units squared |
| **Standard Deviation** | Square root of variance | $$\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$$ | Same unit as data |
| **Interquartile Range (IQR)** | Q3 – Q1 | – | Robust to outliers, middle 50% |

#### 📝 Worked Example – Variance & Std Dev

Using the same heights: [155, 160, 165, 170, 175, 180], mean = 167.5.

| x | deviation (x - mean) | squared deviation |
|---|----------------------|-------------------|
| 155 | -12.5 | 156.25 |
| 160 | -7.5  | 56.25  |
| 165 | -2.5  | 6.25   |
| 170 | +2.5  | 6.25   |
| 175 | +7.5  | 56.25  |
| 180 | +12.5 | 156.25 |
| **Sum** | 0 | 437.50 |

**Population variance** = 437.50 / 6 = **72.92** cm²  
**Population std dev** = √72.92 ≈ **8.54** cm  
**Sample variance** = 437.50 / (6-1) = **87.50** cm²  
**Sample std dev** = √87.50 ≈ **9.35** cm

> 💡 **Why divide by n‑1?** To correct for bias in sample estimates – it provides an unbiased estimate of the population variance.

### 📌 Five‑Number Summary & Box Plot

The five‑number summary gives a quick snapshot of the data:

1. **Minimum**
2. **First Quartile (Q1)** – 25th percentile
3. **Median (Q2)** – 50th percentile
4. **Third Quartile (Q3)** – 75th percentile
5. **Maximum**

**Example** – sorted heights: 155, 160, 165, 170, 175, 180

| Component | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Min       | 155   |
| Q1        | 160   |
| Median    | 167.5 |
| Q3        | 175   |
| Max       | 180   |
| **IQR**   | 15 (175 – 160) |

**Outlier detection**: values < Q1 – 1.5×IQR or > Q3 + 1.5×IQR are considered outliers. In this data, no outliers.

## 🔬 Inferential Statistics

Inferential statistics allow us to **make predictions or inferences** about a population based on a sample. They include:

- **Estimation** (confidence intervals)
- **Hypothesis testing** (decision making)

### 🎯 Hypothesis Testing

A structured process to decide whether a claim about a population is supported by sample data.

**Steps**:
1. Define **Null Hypothesis (H₀)** – the default assumption (e.g., no effect).
2. Define **Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)** – what we want to prove.
3. Choose **significance level (α)** – usually 0.05.
4. Compute a **test statistic** from the sample.
5. Find the **p‑value** – probability of observing the data (or more extreme) if H₀ is true.
6. **Decision**: if p ≤ α, reject H₀; otherwise, fail to reject.

#### 📝 Example – Does a new teaching method improve test scores?

- H₀: mean score with new method = mean score with old method
- H₁: mean score with new method ≠ mean score with old method (two‑tailed)
- α = 0.05
- Collect samples, compute t‑statistic, get p‑value = 0.02
- Since 0.02 < 0.05, reject H₀ → the new method has a statistically significant effect.

---

### 📏 Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval (CI) provides a **range of plausible values** for a population parameter (e.g., mean) with a given level of confidence (e.g., 95%).

**Formula for population mean (σ known):**
$$\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

**Example**: We collected a sample of 100 students’ heights. Sample mean = 170 cm, σ = 10 cm.  
95% CI = 170 ± 1.96*(10/√100) = 170 ± 1.96 → [168.04, 171.96]

We are 95% confident that the true population mean height lies between 168.04 cm and 171.96 cm.

---

### 🧪 Common Statistical Tests

| Test | Purpose | Scenario |
|------|---------|----------|
| **Z‑test** | Compare sample mean to population mean (known σ, large n) | Quality control, large samples |
| **t‑test** | Compare means (unknown σ, small n) | A/B testing, comparing two groups |
| **ANOVA** (F‑test) | Compare means of 3+ groups | Comparing test scores across multiple schools |
| **Chi‑Square** | Test independence of categorical variables | Customer preference vs. gender |

## 👥 Population vs. Sample

| Aspect | Population | Sample |
|--------|------------|--------|
| **Definition** | Entire set of individuals/items of interest | A subset of the population |
| **Size** | Usually large or infinite | Finite, manageable |
| **Parameter** | e.g., μ (population mean), σ² | e.g., x̄ (sample mean), s² |
| **Goal** | To describe the entire group | To estimate population characteristics |

### 📝 Real‑World Examples

| Scenario | Population | Sample |
|----------|------------|--------|
| Average height of students in a school | All students in the school | 50 randomly chosen students |
| Purchasing behaviour of consumers | All consumers in the city | 500 consumers selected |
| Effectiveness of a drug | All patients with the disease | 100 patients from various hospitals |
| Defect rate in manufacturing | All products made that day | 30 randomly picked products |

> **Key point**: Inferential statistics uses the sample to draw conclusions about the population, with a measure of uncertainty (e.g., confidence intervals, p‑values).

## 🎲 Sampling Methods

### ✅ Probability Sampling (Randomised)

Each member has a known, non‑zero probability of being selected. This allows for statistical inference.

| Method | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Simple Random** | Every member equally likely | Random number generator to pick 50 students from a list |
| **Stratified** | Split into homogeneous strata, sample from each | Age groups: 18‑25, 26‑35, 36‑45 – sample 10 from each |
| **Systematic** | Select every k‑th member | Survey every 10th person entering a mall |
| **Cluster** | Select entire groups (clusters) | Randomly pick 5 schools and survey all students in those schools |

### ❌ Non‑Probability Sampling (Non‑random)

Selection is not based on probability; may be biased but sometimes used for exploratory research.

| Method | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Convenience** | Easiest to reach | Survey people at a mall during the afternoon |
| **Judgmental** | Based on researcher’s expertise | Choose domain experts for an interview |
| **Snowball** | Existing subjects refer others | Study rare disease patients by starting with a few known cases |
| **Quota** | Fill quotas for certain groups | Ensure 50% male, 50% female in the sample |

**Selecting the right sampling method** depends on the research question, available resources, and desired precision.

## 📋 Types of Data

Data can be categorised into two broad types:

### 🔹 Qualitative (Categorical) Data

Describes attributes or categories. It can be:

- **Nominal**: No order (e.g., Gender, Country, Colour)
- **Ordinal**: Ordered categories (e.g., Education Level, Satisfaction Rating)

### 🔸 Quantitative (Numerical) Data

Represents counts or measurements. It can be:

- **Discrete**: Countable integers (e.g., Number of children, Defects per item)
- **Continuous**: Any value within a range (e.g., Height, Temperature, Income)

## 📏 Scales of Measurement

The scale determines what statistical operations are meaningful.

### 1️⃣ Nominal Scale

- **Characteristics**: Categories only, no order, no numeric meaning.
- **Operations**: Equality tests, mode, frequency counts.
- **Examples**: Gender (Male/Female), Country, Blood type.

### 2️⃣ Ordinal Scale

- **Characteristics**: Categories have order, but differences are not equal.
- **Operations**: Ordering, median, percentiles, but not arithmetic (mean is not meaningful).
- **Examples**: Education level (High School < Bachelor < Master < Doctorate), Socio‑economic status (Low, Middle, High), Customer satisfaction (Not satisfied, Satisfied, Very satisfied).

### 3️⃣ Interval Scale

- **Characteristics**: Ordered, equal intervals, but **no true zero**.
- **Operations**: Addition/subtraction, mean, standard deviation; ratios are not meaningful.
- **Examples**: Temperature in Celsius/Fahrenheit, IQ scores, Calendar years.
- **Example**: 20°C is not twice as warm as 10°C because 0°C is not the absence of temperature.

### 4️⃣ Ratio Scale

- **Characteristics**: All interval properties plus a **true zero**.
- **Operations**: All arithmetic operations, including ratios.
- **Examples**: Height, Weight, Income, Age, Counts (number of items).

**Comparison Table**:

| Scale | Order | Equal Intervals | True Zero | Meaningful Operations |
|-------|-------|-----------------|-----------|------------------------|
| Nominal | No | No | No | =, ≠ |
| Ordinal | Yes | No | No | =, ≠, <, > |
| Interval | Yes | Yes | No | +, −, mean, std dev |
| Ratio | Yes | Yes | Yes | All (including ×, ÷) |

## 🧪 Statistical Tests – When to Use Which

| Test | Hypothesis | Conditions | Example |
|------|------------|------------|---------|
| **One‑sample t‑test** | Sample mean = specified value | Small sample, unknown σ | Check if average height of students equals 170 cm |
| **Two‑sample t‑test** (independent) | Means of two groups equal | Two independent groups | Compare test scores of boys vs. girls |
| **Paired t‑test** | Means of paired differences = 0 | Same subjects measured twice | Before/after treatment |
| **ANOVA** | All group means equal | Three or more groups | Compare scores across 5 schools |
| **Chi‑Square test of independence** | Variables are independent | Two categorical variables | Gender vs. product preference |
| **Chi‑Square goodness‑of‑fit** | Observed frequencies match expected | One categorical variable | Die is fair? |

## 🐍 Python Implementations

### Descriptive Statistics with `numpy` and `pandas`

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Data: heights
heights = [155, 160, 165, 170, 175, 180]

# Using numpy
mean = np.mean(heights)
median = np.median(heights)
std = np.std(heights, ddof=1)  # sample std
var = np.var(heights, ddof=1)  # sample variance
q1, q3 = np.percentile(heights, [25, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1

print(f"Mean: {mean:.2f}, Median: {median:.2f}")
print(f"Sample Std: {std:.2f}, Sample Var: {var:.2f}")
print(f"Q1: {q1:.2f}, Q3: {q3:.2f}, IQR: {iqr:.2f}")

# Using pandas
df = pd.DataFrame(heights, columns=['height'])
print(df.describe())
```

### T‑test, ANOVA, Chi‑Square with `scipy`

```python
from scipy import stats

# One-sample t-test: is mean = 170?
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_1samp(heights, 170)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f}, p = {p_value:.4f}")

# Two-sample t-test (independent)
group1 = [85, 90, 88, 92, 86]
group2 = [78, 82, 80, 85, 81]
t_stat, p_value = stats.ttest_ind(group1, group2)
print(f"t = {t_stat:.3f}, p = {p_value:.4f}")

# ANOVA (one-way)
group1 = [85, 90, 88]
group2 = [78, 82, 80]
group3 = [95, 92, 94]
f_stat, p_value = stats.f_oneway(group1, group2, group3)
print(f"F = {f_stat:.3f}, p = {p_value:.4f}")

# Chi-square test of independence
observed = [[30, 20], [15, 35]]  # contingency table
chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(observed)
print(f"Chi2 = {chi2:.3f}, p = {p:.4f}")
```

## 💡 Why Statistics Matters for AI/ML

- **Data Understanding**: Before building models, you must understand distributions, outliers, and correlations.

- **Model Validation**: Cross‑validation, hypothesis tests for feature importance.
- **Decision Making**: Confidence intervals and p‑values help in business and scientific decisions.

> 🎯 **Mastering statistics gives you the superpower to question data, validate models, and communicate insights effectively.**
